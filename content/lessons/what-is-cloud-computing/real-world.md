## How real companies use the cloud

### Netflix: moving everything to AWS
In 2008 a major database corruption stopped Netflix from shipping DVDs for three days. Netflix decided that running its own data centres was too risky for a fast-growing business. It moved its systems to AWS step by step and finished in January 2016. Netflix chose the cloud for the reasons in this lesson: reliability, the ability to grow quickly, and freedom to focus engineers on the product rather than on hardware.

### A startup launching a product
A two-person team building an app in Bengaluru can't spend lakhs on servers before knowing whether anyone wants the product. On AWS they can start with small, cheap resources, sometimes within the Free Tier, and grow only when users arrive. If the idea fails, they shut everything down and the bills stop. This is **"trade fixed expense for variable expense"** in practice.

### Seasonal traffic: sales and exam results
E-commerce sites see huge spikes during festival sales. Results portals see huge spikes on the day results are published, then almost nothing. Buying hardware for that one peak day would leave it idle for the rest of the year. With **rapid elasticity**, capacity is added for the spike and removed afterwards.

### Going global
A company in India that wants low-latency service for customers in Europe can deploy the same application to an AWS Region in Europe (for example Frankfurt or Ireland) in hours instead of building a data centre there. This is **"go global in minutes"**.

### What this means for you as a cloud engineer
Companies hire cloud engineers to turn these benefits into reality:
- building systems that **scale** without crashing,
- keeping them **secure**,
- and keeping the **bill under control**, because pay-per-use also means a forgotten resource keeps costing money.

The rest of this academy teaches you exactly those skills.
